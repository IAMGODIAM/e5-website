Miami Neighborhoods · Brownsville

Brownsville

Black families were buying and platting land here in the 1910s. White neighbors burned crosses to stop them. The families stayed.

Where
Miami-Dade County, roughly NW 41st to NW 62nd Street, NW 19th to NW 37th Avenue. Locally, Brown Sub.
Platted
1916, as Brown's Subdivision
Known for
The Historic Hampton House, Lincoln Memorial Park Cemetery, the 2025 legacy murals
Population
16,583 (2020 Census, Brownsville CDP); land area 2.28 square miles

What Brownsville is

Brownsville is an unincorporated neighborhood in Miami-Dade County, northwest of Liberty City and north of Allapattah. It is one of Miami-Dade's earliest Black suburbs, and it is currently changing faster than almost any neighborhood in the county.

Two housekeeping notes, because both cost people time. This is not Brownsville, Texas. And Brownsville is adjacent to Liberty City, not part of it — a distinction that matters most for the Hampton House, which is here.

Brown's Subdivision, 1916

The land was platted in 1916 as Brown's Subdivision, in what was then Allapattah Prairie. Historical records suggest W. L. Brown and his wife Martha Brown were Black farmers and landowners; a 1914 map shows the Williams and Brown families already owning land there. The survey that established this states plainly that more research into their identity is needed.[1]

A contested founding

Accounts of Brownsville's origins disagree by roughly a decade, and on the founders' race. The NPS-funded Historic Brownsville Survey traces Black ownership to 1914–16. Journalistic accounts describe a 1920s farming development that "attracted Black residents in the 1940s."[2][3] One reference work describes it as originally a settlement for white families. We follow the survey, which is the most recent and most documentary of these, while noting it does not consider the question closed.

By 1927 Black property ownership had expanded well beyond the Browns — J.D. Williams, Dr. W.B. Sawyer, Samuel Wilson, F. Ayres and T. Amos among them. Sawyer, a prominent Black physician, platted the Centerville subdivision in 1927 and built Alberta Heights in 1949, described as one of the first Black-owned rental apartment projects in the area.[1]

This was resisted, violently. Between 1940 and 1951 there were reported cross burnings and intimidation in Brownsville as white property owners tried to frighten Black residents away.[1] It did not work.

The mid-century neighborhood

When I-95 construction destroyed Overtown in the 1960s, displaced families and businesses moved here, and Brownsville became majority Black.[4][10] Bethune Elementary opened in 1951, with Mary McLeod Bethune attending the dedication. Bannerman Park and Pool followed in 1969, built as compensation for public housing — between 1965 and 1969 more than a hundred local property owners publicly opposed those housing plans.[1]

Prolonged Metrorail construction along NW 27th Avenue in the 1980s damaged the commercial corridor, and the neighborhood has carried that disinvestment since.[2]

The Hampton House

The Historic Hampton House, at 4240 NW 27th Avenue, was built in 1954 and had Brownsville's first swimming pool. During segregation it was where Black travellers, performers and organisers stayed: Martin Luther King Jr., Muhammad Ali, Malcolm X, Sam Cooke, Aretha Franklin and Jackie Robinson all passed through. It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on 8 February 2023.[5]

The night Ali beat Sonny Liston in 1964 and celebrated with Malcolm X, Sam Cooke and Jim Brown — the night dramatised in One Night in Miami — happened here, in Brownsville. It is frequently and incorrectly placed in Overtown.

Lincoln Memorial Park

Lincoln Memorial Park Cemetery was platted in 1924 and was originally owned by a white developer, F. B. Miller.[1] It is one of the few places Black Miamians could be buried. D.A. Dorsey, Miami's first Black millionaire, is buried there, as is Representative Gwen Cherry.[2]

The murals, 2025

In 2025 a legacy mural project at Jefferson Reaves Sr. Park honoured eleven figures from Brownsville's history: Gwendolyn Sawyer Cherry, the first Black woman elected to the Florida Legislature; Joseph Lang Kershaw; Neal Adams Sr.; Jefferson Reaves Sr.; George W. Kilpatrick Sr.; Bishop J.D. Williams; Dr. Enid C. Pinkney; Everett Stewart Sr.; William Brown; Representative James Bush III; and Dr. Dorothy Jenkins Fields, founder of the Black Archives.[4] Dr. Pinkney died in July 2024 at 92.[6]

Georgette's Tea Room, which drew Billie Holiday in the 1940s and 1950s, and Jackson's Toddle Inn are among the other sites the historic survey documents.[2]

What the 2020 census shows

Brownsville CDP recorded 16,583 residents in the 2020 Census, up from 15,313 in 2010 and 14,393 in 2000, across 2.28 square miles.[7] Median household income is $40,795, the poverty rate 26.2%, and homeownership 35.7%, against a median home value of $325,200.[7]

The composition changed sharply in that decade. The Hispanic population rose 91.4%, from 3,939 to 7,541. The Black population fell 23.8%, from 11,081 to 8,448.[2] We give the raw counts rather than percentages of the whole, because different census tabulations produce materially different shares for the same place.

The Brownsville Transit Village, 5.2 acres beside the Metrorail station at 5115 NW 29th Avenue, has delivered 401 affordable units across four fourteen-story buildings, with Phase V's 120 units breaking ground on 22 July 2024 — 521 in total, at 30–80% of area median income.[8] In 2023 a proposed Hialeah annexation of about 151 acres and 137 mobile homes drew more than a hundred residents in opposition, and was dropped.[9]

What we could not establish

Brownsville is less documented than Liberty City or Overtown, and we would rather say so than pad. We could not find Brownsville-specific population loss figures from highway construction, a Brownsville redlining map, or verified biographical detail on W. L. and Martha Brown. Gwen Cherry is buried here and honoured in the murals, but we could not confirm that she lived here. Two widely circulated county community reports on Brownsville contain a passage deriving the neighborhood's name from the Fontainebleau resort, which is an error carried over from a different neighborhood's profile; we have not used them.
