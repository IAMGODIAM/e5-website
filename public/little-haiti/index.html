Miami Neighborhoods · Little Haiti

Little Haiti

The neighborhood waited forty years for Miami to put its name on a map, and got it in 2016 — just as the ground under it became the most contested in the city.

Where
NE Miami, roughly 54th Street to 79th Street, I-95 east to NE 2nd Avenue
Named
Officially recognised by the City of Miami, 26 May 2016
Known for
The Caribbean Marketplace, Little Haiti Cultural Complex, Sounds of Little Haiti
Population
Roughly 30,000; over 70% Black. Figures shift with where you draw the boundary.

Before it was Little Haiti

The area was Lemon City, settled after the Civil War by African American and Bahamian settlers near Biscayne Bay, founded in 1869 and named for the wild sweet lemon trees growing there. A post office called Motto operated by 1889; the name Lemon City took hold by 1893. When the Florida East Coast Railway extended south in 1896, prominence shifted to Miami proper.[1][2]

The crossing

The first documented Haitian refugee boat, the Saint Sauveur, landed at Pompano Beach on 12 December 1972 carrying 65 passengers.[3] Through the 1970s, people fled the Duvalier regime. Around 25,000 Haitians arrived in 1980 alone.[4]

The Cuban-Haitian Entrant Act of 1980 followed Haitian Refugee Center v. Civiletti, in which a federal court found racial discrimination in how Haitian asylum claims were being decided.[3] Later waves came as gang violence and political collapse deepened, intensifying after the assassination of President Jovenel Moïse in 2021.[3]

The name

Viter Juste (1924–2012), a businessman and organiser, proposed calling the area Little Port-au-Prince. Miami Herald editors shortened it to Little Haiti in a headline, and the short version stuck.[5]

It took another three decades to become official. On 26 May 2016 the Miami City Commission voted unanimously to recognise Little Haiti as a neighborhood, on a resolution sponsored by Commissioner Keon Hardemon.[6][7] The vote was contested — some property owners preferred Lemon City, Little River or Buena Vista.[7]

The institutions

  • Caribbean Marketplace (Maché Ayisyen) — 5925 NE 2nd Avenue. Opened 1990, designed by Charles Harrison Pawley after Haiti's gingerbread houses and the Marché en Fer, the Iron Market in Port-au-Prince. It won a Florida Architect Award and an AIA National Honor Award. It closed in 1999; the city acquired it in 2005 and planned to demolish it; community protest saved it, and it reopened in the mid-2010s.[8][9] It closed again in June 2024 for roof and mould repairs, with $5.3 million allocated.[9]
  • Little Haiti Cultural Complex — 212–260 NE 59th Terrace. Championed by the late Commissioner Arthur E. Teele Jr.; a 300-seat theater, a 2,150 square foot gallery, dance studios, and roughly 100,000 visitors a year.[10][11]
  • Notre-Dame d'Haiti Catholic Church — the Pierre Toussaint Haitian Catholic Center was founded in January 1980; the parish was established in August 1981 in the former Notre Dame Academy for Girls, with its opening Mass that November. Fr. Thomas Wenski, later Archbishop of Miami, joined the Haitian ministry in 1979 and published Lavwa Katolik, described as the first Creole-language newspaper in the United States.[12]
  • Libreri Mapou — 5919 NE 2nd Avenue, the Creole and French bookstore founded by Jan Mapou, who co-founded Mouvman Kreýol Ayisyen in 1965, was imprisoned at Fort Dimanche by the Duvalier regime in 1969, and went on to found Sosyete Koukouy.[13]
Dates we could not pin down

Sources disagree on when the Little Haiti Cultural Complex opened (2006 groundbreaking versus 2009 opening), when Libreri Mapou was founded (1987 or 1990), and exactly when the Caribbean Marketplace reopened. We have left those unresolved rather than pick the version that reads best. Sant La Haitian Neighborhood Center, often listed as a Little Haiti institution, is headquartered in North Miami.[14]

The culture

Kreyòl is spoken in the streets, the restaurants and the markets; Haitian Creole is Florida's third most-spoken language, with roughly 426,000 speakers at home.[15] The music is kompa. The food is diri ak djon djon, black mushroom rice; griot, fried pork; pikliz, the spicy cabbage slaw; pen kreýol.[16]

Sounds of Little Haiti runs on the third Friday of the month at the Cultural Complex, launched in 2016 after Big Night in Little Haiti ended for lack of funding.[17] The Haitian Compas Festival was founded by Rodney Noel and Cecibon Production in the late 1990s, though recent editions have been held outside the neighborhood.[18]

The pressure

Greater Miami is home to more than 300,000 people of Haitian ancestry.[19] Little Haiti itself is around 30,000 people and has been roughly flat since 2000 — but the composition has shifted. An FIU study cited by The World found family households down 13.5% since 2000, the "White alone" population up 49.6%, and "some other race" up 69.1%.[19] Housing stock in Little Haiti grew at four times the countywide rate.[20]

Climate gentrification — a real argument, and a contested one

Little Haiti sits on the northern edge of a limestone ridge, roughly 10 to 17 feet above sea level, in a county where 56% of the land is six feet or less.[21] The 2018 study Climate gentrification: from theory to empiricism in Miami-Dade County analysed 107,984 single-family homes and found that properties at 2–4 metres elevation appreciated faster than those at 0–1 metre, especially after 2000. That paper names Little Haiti explicitly.[22]

It is not settled. The geographer Han Li argues the effect cannot be cleanly separated from ordinary gentrification: "You cannot distinguish the Magic City project in Little Haiti from the traditional gentrification process in Wynwood."[21] We present it as a live argument, because that is what it is.

The Magic City Innovation District was approved by the Miami City Commission in June 2019 on a 3–0 vote after five public hearings and organised opposition: fifteen acres between NE 60th and 64th Streets, a billion dollars, 2,670 apartments up to 25 stories, and $31 million committed to a new Little Haiti Revitalization Trust.[23] The first $3 million instalment was paid in 2021, with activists criticising how the money would be distributed.[24] In June 2026 the project was revived as a $3 billion, 7.8 million square foot AI-focused district, with the $31 million trust commitment reaffirmed.[25]
