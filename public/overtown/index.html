Miami Neighborhoods · Overtown

Overtown

Black laborers cast roughly four in ten of the votes that made Miami a city. Sixty years later the state ran an interstate through the neighborhood they built.

Where
Just northwest of downtown Miami. The Miami-Dade MPO draws it NW 20th Street to NW 5th Street, I-95 to the FEC railway.
Founded
1896, with the City of Miami itself
Known for
The Lyric Theater, "the Harlem of the South," the Historic Overtown Folklife Village
Population
8,601 — the most recent official figure, from 2010 Census and 2015 ACS data

Miami does not exist without Overtown

Miami incorporated on 28 July 1896. To do it, the city needed a minimum number of registered voters, and it did not have them. Black laborers — mostly Bahamian immigrants and migrants from the American South, recruited by Henry Flagler's railroad — were registered to make up the difference. Of the 368 voters present, 162 were Black.[1][2]

They were disenfranchised almost immediately afterward, sent back to work and stripped of further political power.[3] Restrictive deed covenants then barred Black residents from buying land anywhere in Miami except this one northwest sector — roughly fifteen per cent of the city's original area.[1]

It was called Colored Town, then officially the Central Negro District, then renamed Washington Heights in 1937. The name Overtown came from residents: people from Coconut Grove said they were going "over town."[4] That explanation comes from oral history rather than a document — the historian Paul George found no use of the name in primary sources from 1896 to 1930.[1]

The Harlem of the South

From the 1930s to the 1950s, NW 2nd Avenue was Little Broadway. The Lyric Theater, built in 1913 by Geder Walker, a Black businessman from Georgia, seated 400. In 1915 the Miami Metropolis called it "possibly the most beautiful and costly playhouse owned by colored people in all the Southland."[5][6] Walker died in 1919; his widow Henrietta ran the theater until 1959. The Black Archives acquired it in 1988, it was listed on the National Register in 1989, and it reopened in 2000.[6]

Count Basie, Ella Fitzgerald, Billie Holiday, Nat King Cole, Duke Ellington, Sam Cooke, Aretha Franklin, Josephine Baker and B.B. King all performed in Overtown.[7][8] Many of them were playing whites-only rooms on Miami Beach the same week — and were not allowed to sleep there. They came back across the tracks at night, to the Sir John Hotel's Knight Beat, the Mary Elizabeth, the Rockland Palace.[7]

This was a functioning economy, not just a nightlife district. By 1915, when Miami had 15,592 residents of whom 5,659 were Black, Black-held property in the city was valued at roughly $800,000.[9] D.A. Dorsey, Miami's first Black millionaire, paid for the community's first park, library and school.[1]

The highways

Miami's 1955 expressway plan routed the road along an abandoned rail corridor, avoiding neighborhoods. After federal interstate funding arrived in 1956, the Florida State Road Department's consultants scrapped that plan and moved the route west, straight through Overtown, "to provide ample room for the future expansion of the central business district."[10]

No public hearing was held in Overtown.[11] A Miami planner described the goal as "a complete slum clearance effectively removing every Negro family from the present city limits." A 1961 Miami Herald headline read: "He'd Shift Negro District, Build New 'Downtown'."[10]

The scale, and why we give a range

Overtown's population fell from roughly 40,000 around 1960 to well under 10,000 by the end of the 1960s. Estimates of the peak run from 30,000 to 50,000 depending on the source, and the "after" figure is given as 8,000 or 10,000.[10][12][13] The historian N.D.B. Connolly puts the number of displaced households at 8,500.[14]

The I-95 and I-395 interchange alone consumed forty square blocks, including housing for about 10,000 people.[10][11] I-395 and SR 836 then cut what was left into four pieces.[15] We publish the range rather than pick a number, because the sources genuinely disagree and the disagreement is itself part of the record.

What is happening now

In July 2024 Overtown received a $39 million HUD Choice Neighborhoods grant inside a $332 million plan. Culmer Gardens and Culmer Place are expanding from 226 units to 1,056, of which 621 are affordable and 212 market-rate. An $84 million park, the Underdeck, is planned beneath I-395.[16]

The concern that comes with it is displacement. Catalyst Miami notes that many Overtown households earn under $12,000 a year.[16] As in Liberty City, elevation is part of the pressure: Overtown sits on high ground, which has made it a target as sea-level risk gets priced into Miami real estate.[17]

The last official population count available for the neighborhood is 8,601, from the Miami-Dade MPO using 2010 Census and 2015 ACS data. That report also records per-capita income of $11,578 against $28,823 countywide.[15] We could not find a reliable post-2020 neighborhood-level population figure, and we are not going to invent one.

Where to see it

  • Historic Lyric Theater — 819 NW 2nd Avenue. Built 1913, National Register 1989.[5][6]
  • Black Police Precinct and Courthouse Museum — dedicated May 1950 at a cost of $60,000, the segregated headquarters for Miami's Black officers and its Black judge, and unique in the Southeast. Lawson E. Thomas was the first Black judge; the precinct had 79 Black officers by the time it was abolished in 1963.[18]
  • Historic Overtown Folklife Village — NW 2nd and 3rd Avenues between 8th and 10th Streets, designated by the State of Florida as the Overtown Main Street Community.[19]
  • Greater Bethel AME (Martin Luther King Jr. spoke there in 1958), Mt. Zion Missionary Baptist (1896), St. Agnes Episcopal (1898), St. John's Baptist.[13]
  • The D.A. Dorsey House, the Ward Rooming House, and Purvis Young's murals.[20]
Two things that are commonly got wrong

The Historic Hampton House — where Muhammad Ali, Malcolm X, Sam Cooke and Jim Brown spent the night in 1964 — is not in Overtown. It is at 4240 NW 27th Avenue in Brownsville.[21] Plenty of travel pages put it here.

The National Register "Overtown Historic District" is in Sarasota, not Miami. Miami's Overtown has individually listed properties, including the Lyric, but no federally listed district of that name.

1982

On 28–30 December 1982, unrest followed the shooting of Nevell Johnson Jr. by officer Luis Alvarez in an Overtown arcade. One person was killed, 26 injured, 43 arrested. Alvarez was acquitted in March 1984.[22] Further unrest followed in 1989. The 1980 McDuffie uprising, often conflated with this, centred on Liberty City — we cover it there.
