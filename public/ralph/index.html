I · 327 Northwest Fifteenth StreetTranscript, p. 23–24

“Even when we went to sleep, my parents left the light on the front porch.”

Ralph McCartney · August 14, 1997

His parents, Leon McCartney, Sr. and Lillian Ferguson McCartney, were both born in Eleuthera, in the Bahamas. All eight of their children were born in Overtown. Ralph was the last of them, born March 11, 1934.

The family rented an eight-room house at 327 Northwest Fifteenth Street, four rooms to the front and four to the back, with a porch running the length of it. Mrs. Turner, the midwife who lived across the street, delivered all eight children.

On the porch there were card tables and checkerboards. Anyone passing could sit down and play, day or night. The only rule was not to wake the family.

When other people held record hops for school, the McCartneys sometimes had a seven- to eleven-piece band playing in the living room. The kids at school called it the McCartney Palace on McCartney Square, after the Rockland Palace and Harlem Square nightclubs.

In 1997 he named the neighborhood building by building. Kelly’s Barber Shop, where Jake Thompkins cut his hair. The Chop Suey on Second Avenue, with the best shrimp fried rice he ever tasted. The Harlem Theater, where Black Miami went to the movies. When the Capitol Theater downtown was torn down, its sign was hung on the Harlem and the Harlem was renamed the Capitol, to save a sign.

Every morning, Howard Johnson rode past the house on his bicycle calling his nickname, “R.C.”, and Ralph climbed onto the handlebars to be carried to school.

When police came to harass the young men at Peck’s Ice Cream Parlor, Mr. Peck told them: “This is my place of business, you don’t bother with them, they are my customers.”

“We had a beautiful family life, and the whole neighborhood was a family.”

Transcript, p. 2–3

The teachers and principals, he said, “were like extended members of the family.” Going to school was what was cool. Friendships lasted a lifetime.

III · Interstate 95 · Interstate 395 · State Road 836

Then came the Big Monster.

“It was like somebody ripped my guts apart, because I saw me destroyed. Being a product of Overtown, I was Overtown.”

Transcript, p. 41–42 · on first seeing what I-95 had done
WILLIAMSDo you know what your parents were paid for the home by the state?
McCARTNEYThe state gave us nothing.
WILLIAMSDid you receive any relocation money?
McCARTNEYNone whatsoever.

He was in New York when the interstate came. Home on vacation, his oldest brother Leon drove him out one night to see Overtown. After a while Ralph asked when they would get there. “Man, we almost in the Grove,” Leon said. They had already driven through it. He hadn’t recognized a thing.

The expressways cut the community into four pieces. Then the school board moved to phase out Booker T. Washington, the school he called the catalyst of everything Overtown had been.

“They gave us Booker Washington and they made us proud, and they took it away and they destroyed us.”

Transcript, p. 39

V · CyranoTranscript, p. 7–8 · 12

A poet’s tongue. A programmer’s mind.

After Booker T. Washington, class of 1952, and a stint in the United States Army, he went to live with his brother Carl in New York. He worked for the Postal Service, then went to Chase Manhattan Bank as a data clerk and computer programmer. He had learned to program the IBM 407.

He was offered a salary to teach white salesmen the machine he knew, so they could earn fifty or sixty thousand dollars a year selling it while he earned about a hundred a week. “I just quietly walked away,” he said.

At the bank they told him they didn’t use the 407 and made him a proof machine operator. His first morning at One Chase Manhattan Plaza, he rode the elevator four basements down. The first thing he saw when the doors opened was an IBM 407.

Bob Simms, who ran the Community Relations Board, called him Cyrano because of his poetic background. Congresswoman Meek told the House he was best known for his eloquence in reciting great works of literature. His family remembers his baritone.

VI · The workTranscript, p. 7–18 · Obituary · Proclamation

He was needed here.

The City of Opa-locka put it plainly in 1994: his career took him to New York City, where he joined the civil rights campaigns of Dr. Martin Luther King, Jr., “but returned to his hometown, Miami, because he realized he was needed here.”

1934

Born in Overtown

March 11. The youngest of eight children of Leon and Lillian McCartney. A lifelong member of St. Peter’s African Orthodox Church, where he and his brothers served as acolytes.

1952

Booker T. Washington, Class of ’52

He graduated under principal Charles L. Williams, a legend in this town. The same year Urban Renewal pushed the family out of Overtown. Then the United States Army.

1950s

New York City

The Postal Service, then Chase Manhattan Bank as a data clerk and computer programmer. Involved in politics and in the civil rights campaigns of Dr. Martin Luther King, Jr.

Early ’60s

Home to Miami

Projectionist, then Zip Press on Forty-Sixth Street and Seventh Avenue, where he learned the printing business and came to manage it.

1960s–70s

The organizer

Credit union manager for the Economic Opportunity Program (EOPI). Director of the Neighborhood Service Center in South Miami. The Urban League. The Community Relations Board under Robert Simms. The Concentrated Employment Program. The Opportunities Industrialization Center. Legislative aide in the Florida House of Representatives.

Testimony

The Johnny Jones case

He testified. They black balled me, he said, and he was out of work for two and a half years until Dewey Knight, Jr. sent him across the street to a job.

1980

Photographed in Miami

The portrait on this page, held today in the Southern Labor Archives at Georgia State University, in the records of the Southern Conference Educational Fund.

Later years

Dade County Corrections

He had spent his life trying to keep young people out of jail. Now he met some of them inside it. I must have worked in the jail for at least 7 or 8 months before I learned to stop crying every night. He kept counseling them after they got out.

1994

Honored twice

In the United States House of Representatives on February 1. By the City of Opa-locka, which proclaimed May 25, 1994 “Mr. Ralph McCartney Day.”

1997

Tell the Story

August 14. Devon Williams interviews him at 280 NW 46th Street for the Black Archives and FIU oral history of Overtown, preserved by the University of Florida.

1998

Booker T. Washington Senior High

One of the loudest and most constant voices in the campaign to make Booker T. Washington a senior high school again. The goal was realized in 1998.

2008

September 6

“In the whisper of a moment,” he joined his parents, Leon and Lillian, and his siblings Vera, Leon, Carl and Rudolph.

VII · Three things that will lastAs named by Rep. Carrie P. Meek on the House floor

Concrete. Steel. A school.

i

The Edison Park Elementary I-95 Overpass

“As a result of the relentless efforts of Mr. McCartney, the Edison Park Elementary I-95 Overpass was constructed. Young children no longer have to face the death-defying temptations of taking a short-cut across I-95 to get to school.”

Rep. Carrie P. Meek · Congressional Record, February 1, 1994
ii

The U.S. Department of Defense Race Relations Institute

“Because of the success of this program, those individuals who came through the Miami experience have a much more significant appreciation and understanding of their role in making America and the military a better place for all.”

He also spent weekends with visiting military groups on race relations. It was on one of those weekends that he showed a group Good Bread Alley.

Rep. Carrie P. Meek · Congressional Record · Transcript, p. 42
iii

The rebuilding of Booker T. Washington

“The tireless work of Mr. McCartney, the late Dr. Johnny Jones, the school board, and the community, led to the successful rebuilding of the school.”

He served on the committee that reviewed the school’s future. One reason given for phasing it out was that it stood too close to an expressway. “They built the expressway near Booker Washington, and at the same time they wanted to phase Booker Washington out, they were building Edison next to an existing expressway. Now, you tell me the justification for it.”

Congressional Record · Transcript, p. 40
VIII · 1994The House of Representatives · The City of Opa-locka

Honored in Congress. Honored at home.

February 1, 1994CONGRESSIONAL RECORD — HOUSE

TRIBUTE TO MR. RALPH McCARTNEY

(Mrs. MEEK asked and was given permission to address the House for 1 minute and to revise and extend her remarks.)

Mrs. MEEK. Mr. Speaker, I rise to pay tribute to Mr. Ralph McCartney, who resides in Overtown, FL in the 17th Congressional District of Florida. Mr. McCartney, a community activist, is the last of eight children born to Lillian and Leon McCartney. He has been and is a major contributor to the community of Overtown. Mr. McCartney may be best known for his eloquence in reciting great works of literature, however, his most outstanding contributions are from working behind the scenes.

There is so much more that I can say about Mr. Ralph McCartney; however, his untiring drive to make our community a better place speaks volumes. When asked how he wants to be remembered, he replies, “That I was a McCartney.”

A COMMUNITY OF BROTHERHOOD AND SISTERHOOD

Proclamation
WHEREAS:Community Activist, “MR. RALPH McCARTNEY” was born on March 11, 1934, the last of eight children, his early life was influenced by the richness of his family and the community of “Overtown”; and
WHEREAS:His career has taken him to New York City where he became actively involved in politics and civil rights campaigns, joining the efforts of Dr. Martin Luther King, Jr., but returned to his hometown, Miami, because he realized he was needed here; and
WHEREAS:Even though Mr. McCartney is best known for his fiery oratory and/or his eloquence in reciting great works of literature, his most outstanding contributions of working behind the scenes with elected and appointed officials have gone unheralded; and
WHEREAS:Some of his many accomplishments are: The I-95 overpass constructed so that the children of Edison Park Elementary could avoid the dangers of the interstate and also his relentless efforts with the late Dr. Johnny Jones, the school board and the community in preserving the Booker T. Washington School.

NOW, THEREFORE, I, Robert B. Ingram, Mayor, along with my colleagues of the City Commission: Vice Mayor Steven A. Barrett, Commissioners Helen L. Miller, Ollie B. Kelley and Mary E. Allen, do hereby proclaim, Wednesday, May 25, 1994 as:

“MR. RALPH McCARTNEY DAY”

for his tireless efforts in improving the lives of everyone in this community and for unwavering love for humankind.

Robert B. Ingram, Ph.D.
Mayor, City of Opa-locka

“His most outstanding contributions are from working behind the scenes.”

Mayor Ingram, with Vice Mayor Barrett and Commissioners Miller, Kelley and Allen, proclaimed the day in Opa-locka, on letterhead that reads A Community of Brotherhood and Sisterhood. Three months earlier, the first Black member of Congress from Florida since Reconstruction had entered his name into the permanent record of the United States.

IX · In his own voiceTranscript, p. 13 · 56–57

“You’re an individual, you’re a human being, and when you see something wrong, you’ve got to speak out against it. Because if you don’t, then you’re perpetuating that wrong thing.”

Transcript, p. 13
a crumb“We fought for awhile and then they gave us a crumb.”
a slice“We fought a little harder and they gave us a slice, and we stopped fighting.”
the loaf“Somewhere in existence there is a loaf that we didn’t even see.”

He did not let his own generation off the hook. Those who had retired, he said, should be volunteer hall monitors in the schools and fixtures at every City Commission, County Commission, School Board and Zoning Board meeting. “Instead of what the system ought do, what should we demand that the system do.”

And he wanted the proof of Black Miami built where anyone could see it, so that any Black child, “be 7 or 70, should be able to point it out and say that’s me, I did that. Not personally, but mine ancestors did that.”

X · The Overtown he wanted back

“Where I could just see kids out on the park playing … and that every child belongs to them, and that every child realizes that he has a parent in every adult.”

Transcript, p. 58 · the interview’s final answer
“Hey Joe!”“Hey Mary!”“What’s going on?”

“You come worship with me this Sunday and I’ll come worship with you the following Sunday. That’s what I would like to see, that’s the Overtown I grew up in. That’s what I would like to see again.”

The light is still on.

After Overtown, his father found a home on Sixty-fourth Street. “Where we have been ever since,” Ralph said in 1997, and he gave its address: 820 Northwest Sixty-fourth Street. He kept it as his home address wherever his work took him, because he wanted to live among the people he served.

It is the address of E5 Enclave today, the institution his grandnephew Israel founded to carry this work forward.
